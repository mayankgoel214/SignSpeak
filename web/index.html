SignSpeak

American Sign Language fingerspelling, recognised in this tab. Your webcam feed is read by MediaPipe, reduced to 21 hand landmarks, and classified by a 52,000-parameter neural network. No frame of video leaves your device — there is no server to send it to.

Your browser will ask permission. Nothing is recorded or uploaded.

    What the accuracy number means

    Most published sign-language accuracy figures are measured on a random split of the data. Public ASL datasets are frames pulled from a handful of continuous recording sessions, so neighbouring frames are near-duplicates of one another. Split them randomly and almost every test image has a near-twin in training; the model scores beautifully and then falls apart on a stranger's hand. Both numbers are below, because the contrast is the honest answer.

    The 24 letters

    Each hand below is a real pose from the training data — the sample closest to the average of its letter — drawn from its landmarks. It is what the model has actually learned each letter to look like, rather than an illustration of what it should look like.

    How it works

    1. MediaPipe finds the hand. It returns 21 landmarks — knuckles, joints, fingertips — as 3D coordinates. This is the expensive part, and it runs on your GPU.
    2. The pose is normalised. The wrist becomes the origin, the hand is rotated so it points up, and it is scaled so its farthest landmark sits at distance 1. Left hands are mirrored onto right. What survives is shape alone — not where the hand was, how big it was, how it was tilted, or which hand it was.
    3. A small network reads the shape. 63 numbers into two hidden layers into 24 letters. It never sees a pixel, which is exactly why it does not learn a particular person's skin, room or lighting.
    4. A letter is committed when it holds. Roughly half a second of a steady, confident sign adds one letter, so the buffer does not fill with the poses your hand passed through on the way.

    Where it fails

    Worth knowing before you decide the demo is broken, and taken from the measured confusion matrix rather than from intuition. R is the worst letter at 70.5%, and it goes to U one time in five: crossed fingers and two raised fingers are nearly the same landmark set. E drifts to O, N to M, and K to R or G.

    P and Q are a self-inflicted wound, and the interesting one. The two signs are close to the same hand shape at a different orientation, and the normalisation step deliberately rotates orientation away — which is precisely what makes the model work on a stranger whose wrist sits at a different angle. The generalisation and the P/Q confusion are the same design decision seen from two sides.

    J and Z do not exist here at all: both are drawn in the air, and this model sees one frame at a time. And a webcam pointed at a face, a wall, or two hands at once gives MediaPipe nothing to work with, at which point the readout shows a dash rather than guessing. The full per-letter breakdown is in eval/results.json.
